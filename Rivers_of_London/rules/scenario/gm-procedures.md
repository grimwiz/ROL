# GM Procedures

<!-- Source: policing-and-gm-procedures, printed pages 218-230. Status: extracted-draft. -->

This file covers GM-facing procedure for tone, preparation, player agency, pacing, clues, props, technology, and table issues.

## GM Role

The GM is not the players' opponent. NPCs may oppose the investigators, but the table is collaboratively creating a case story.

Core GM duties:

- Understand the core rules well enough to make confident rulings.
- Prepare or learn the case file.
- Keep the case logically coherent.
- Give the investigators clues they can act on.
- Help the players invest in their investigators and the setting.
- Arbitrate rules and table disagreements when needed.
- Keep the investigators, not powerful NPCs, at the centre of the story.

Core player duties:

- Engage with the investigation.
- Keep notes.
- Ask questions.
- Follow leads.
- Roleplay their investigators with enough focus that the case can move.

## Tone

Every case is a detective investigation, but the tone can range from cosy crime to dark modern crime. For the book-series feel, keep the following defaults.

### Not Gritty

The default world is an idealised crime-fiction version of London.

Use these tone rules:

- The Met are broadly the good guys from the viewpoint of the stories.
- Individual officers may be difficult, obstructive, or corrupt, but the system is not defaulted to hopeless corruption.
- Budget and bureaucracy are obstacles, not campaign-ending collapse.
- Keep the police professional unless the group has chosen a different tone.
- Avoid or handle with extreme caution plots built around child abuse, sexual violence, or degradation.
- Punch up. Cruel major villains should usually be powerful, privileged, wealthy, inhuman, or otherwise above ordinary victims.
- Keep a generally optimistic view of humanity.
- Treat personhood, rights, and dignity broadly, including for non-human beings where appropriate.
- Treat marginalised groups with respect and specificity. Research culture rather than using stereotypes.

### Not Horror

The setting has monsters, danger, murder, and frightening magic, but it is not nihilistic horror by default.

Use these defaults:

- Community, family, and place matter.
- Many supernatural beings can be contained, negotiated with, understood, or folded into society.
- A case should usually be solved satisfactorily.
- If a case ends badly, it should support a larger later victory rather than negate the investigators' efforts.
- Failed pushed rolls can be embarrassing, comic, costly, or complicated without always becoming catastrophic.
- If borrowing horror material, deliberately decide whether the tone is shifting toward horror or back toward Rivers of London optimism.

### Spirit Of Place

The setting is animist rather than theistic. Supernatural entities generally emanate from places, rivers, histories, institutions, and local patterns.

Use these limits:

- Genii locorum and river beings are local spirits, not universal gods.
- Large abstract deities should not appear as default literal beings.
- A local entity might claim or resemble a larger mythic identity without being that universal deity.
- Place should matter to plot, vestigia, NPC behaviour, and supernatural logic.

## Running A Published Case

A published case is the easiest starting point.

Preparation checklist:

- Read the case at least twice.
- First read: understand plot shape, major NPCs, crisis points, tone, pacing, likely session count, break points, success conditions, and whether the investigators drive events or react to antagonist pressure.
- Second read: mark clues, skill rolls, key dialogue, important NPC facts, page references, and scenes that need quick lookup.
- Check whether the clue chain makes sense.
- Move or duplicate clues if players are likely to miss them.
- Add details, incidents, or alternate clue routes to fit the group or campaign.
- Look up real places used by the case so descriptions are concrete.

Use bookmarks, sticky tabs, or separate notes until rules and tables are easy to find.

### Scene Presentation

Keep initial descriptions short and concrete. Give the broad picture, then let players ask questions. Q&A often gives players a clearer mental image than a long read-aloud block.

For melee or confused physical scenes, use a sketch map or simple tokens to establish relative position, hazards, exits, and who is engaged with whom. The combat system is narrative, but shared spatial understanding prevents frustration.

### NPC Handling

Keep a list of spare names for incidental NPCs.

If you need quick NPC skill numbers:

- Expert skills often start around `60%`.
- Common and combat skills often start around `30%`.
- Set values to fit occupation and story role.
- If uncertain, use `50%`.

Use investigator backstories as hooks, information sources, emotional stakes, and recurring complications.

## Player Agency

Players are not listening to a novel. Their decisions, deductions, rolls, roleplay, and good ideas should change how the case unfolds.

During Session Zero and character creation:

- Tell the players what kind of cases the campaign supports.
- Encourage skill specialisation so the team has different areas of competence.
- Establish how police and civilian investigators work together.
- Decide whether the team is Folly police, mixed consultants, civilians loosely attached to the Folly, or something else.

During play:

- Use rolls from varied skills so different investigators matter.
- Watch for quiet players and ask them open questions.
- Keep NPC backup in the background when possible.
- If powerful NPCs must be present, give the investigators meaningful decisions and rolls.

### Police Hierarchy And Civilian Investigators

Law-enforcement investigators are constrained by hierarchy, procedure, and public legality. Folly investigators have more latitude because secrecy and demi-monde diplomacy often make ordinary prosecution impossible.

Use this tension for roleplay, but do not let realism make civilian investigators irrelevant. Gameplay wins over strict procedure. Civilians should have autonomy and real risks if their players want that.

### Nightingale And Other Powerful NPCs

Nightingale and Peter can easily solve too much if overused.

Keep them as resources, not solutions:

- Make them busy, distant, injured, politically constrained, or dealing with other threats.
- Let them answer narrow questions or ask focusing questions.
- Do not let calling them become the default answer to danger.
- Hold high-level practitioner antagonists until the investigators are ready, or keep them indirect and cautious.

## Group Size

The game works best with `2-4` players and one GM. It can support more, but the rulebook suggests no more than `6` players plus the GM.

Small groups are easier to pace and focus. Large groups benefit from wider skill coverage and more varied roles, but need more spotlight management.

One-to-one play works well for a Peter Grant-style viewpoint. Give the lone investigator advice and support, but avoid physical jeopardy that removes the only player from action. Nightingale can be nearby, but must not take over.

Useful civilian or specialist investigator types:

- Consulting detective or private investigator.
- Medium, psychic, hedge witch, or similar Folly irregular.
- Criminal psychologist or profiler.
- Scientific consultant.
- Criminal underworld contact or informant.
- Community consultant with local or demi-monde ties.
- Digital forensics expert.
- Historian, archivist, lawyer, stage magician, priest, or other occupation with useful contacts or skills.

## Flashbacks

If the team's Folly connection is left vague during Session Zero, a later flashback case can show how the investigators met the Folly and formed the team.

Use flashbacks to deepen roleplay, but preserve copies of original character sheets if the flashback occurs before magic training or other later development. Avoid lethal danger in flashbacks unless the campaign already supports paradox or retcon handling.

## Accuracy Versus Play

Do not over-explain magic physics. Keep magical rules consistent enough to play.

For mundane science and forensics, accuracy can bend for pacing:

- Urgent DNA matching can take roughly `8` hours from lab delivery.
- Ordinary DNA results may take `24-72` hours or longer.
- A backlog can delay information when the plot needs time.
- A fast result can keep momentum when the plot needs action.

Keep changes subtle and consistent. If a campaign establishes that a database or forensic shortcut works one way, do not contradict that later without an in-world reason.

Police realism should support play, not stall it. Grant resources, warrants, access, and specialist support quickly when the story needs them. Do not simulate paperwork unless the paperwork is the point of the scene.

## Pacing

Keep players engaged. If they are enjoying character interaction rather than pushing the plot, that can still be useful play. If a scene is dragging and no longer producing fun or information, end it once the important exchange is done.

Useful pacing tools:

- Ask whether anyone has final questions before cutting away.
- Skip downtime until the next result arrives.
- Summarise repetitive police work instead of roleplaying every interview.
- Present only the important part of a witness conversation.
- Keep all players involved even if real police procedure would split them into pairs.
- Move between split-party scenes on clues, twists, or decisions.

When summarising legwork, make the key clue stand out while still giving enough colour that the work feels real.

## Missing Clues

If players are stuck, diagnose why:

- They missed an `Observation` or `Research` roll.
- They forgot a clue already in their notes.
- They interpreted a clue incorrectly.
- The clue chain has become too narrow.
- They are hesitant because they do not know police procedure.

Recovery options:

- Remind them they can review crime scene photos, samples, interview notes, audio, and video.
- Let an in-character note check surface a missed pattern.
- Have an NPC with records access point out a discrepancy.
- Use the Exhibits Officer, lab staff, or data entry clerk to bring evidence back into view.
- Use Nightingale or a briefing scene to ask focusing questions.
- Let an `INT` roll connect dots, with a complication on failure.
- Let the antagonist act again, creating a new scene and new evidence.

Essential clues should not disappear forever because of one failed roll.

## Props And Tech

Props are optional but useful for players who think visually.

Good low-effort props:

- Location photos.
- NPC portraits.
- Sketch maps.
- Evidence-board cards.
- Letters, reports, emails, or text-message excerpts.
- Key objects as drawings or physical stand-ins.

Tech can enhance investigation:

- Let players research real locations or history in character.
- Send useful URLs or documents after successful `Research` rolls.
- Use satellite/street-view imagery for access routes, witnesses, class signals, and neighbourhood texture.
- Use virtual tours of museums, galleries, or public buildings.
- Use audio clips for voicemails, phone taps, or interview tapes.
- Send in-world emails or reports during or between sessions.
- Use lighting, sound, or a live call from a helper NPC if it serves the scene.

Guard against spoilers, contradiction, and time sinks. If public records contradict the plot, the Folly can have hidden or altered records.

## Unexpected Player Actions

When players do something valid that the case did not anticipate, try to incorporate it before blocking it.

Useful response:

- Ask whether the action can fit the plot without breaking it.
- Say yes with a complication when possible.
- Turn premature success into a new consequence.
- Let an antagonist escape, be a front, use misdirection, or be replaced only if it is fair and coherent.
- If the players truly solved or defeated something early, accept it and move the case consequences forward.
- Call a break if you need time to think.
- End a session on a cliffhanger if the decision needs careful handling.

Do not make preparation so rigid that the players feel railroaded.

## Player Issues

Most table problems are mismatched expectations, play style, or nervousness.

Use the `4Es`:

- Engage: ask why the player is acting that way.
- Explain: state why it is a problem for the table or investigation.
- Encourage: offer a route back into the game.
- Enforce: apply in-character or table consequences only when needed.

In-character enforcement can include police discipline, desk duty, dismissal, arrest, or Professional Standards attention. Out-of-character cooperation is better when possible.

If a player wants a different style of investigator after seeing the campaign in motion, work with them to retire, reassign, or replace the character.

For passive or unsure players, make out-of-character questions explicitly safe:

- What are my options?
- What would normal police procedure be?
- What are my chances?
- What would my investigator know?

If a player makes others uncomfortable or keeps testing boundaries, address it promptly. The GM is responsible for maintaining table safety.

## Boundaries

Discuss tone and limits before play, but recognise that players may not know every boundary in advance or may not want to disclose personal reasons.

Operational guidance:

- Do not assume a detail is harmless because it seems harmless to you.
- If you cross a line, apologise, back off, and redirect.
- Boundaries can change with mood and life circumstances.
- Use safety tools if the group wants them or if uncertainty is high.
- Respect player enjoyment over fidelity to the prepared plot.
- Keep challenge and consequence, but tune them to the table's actual mood.

Mutual respect is required. Players should trust the GM not to arbitrarily kill, block, or waste them; the GM should respect player agency, enjoyment, and achievement.
