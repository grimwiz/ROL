# Policing And Investigations

<!-- Source: policing-and-gm-procedures, printed pages 195-217. Status: reviewed-complete (PDF-checked 2026-05-23; see rules/tracking/pdf-review.md). -->

This file is a scenario-facing reference for Met structure, Folly placement, criminal investigation procedure, evidence, police powers, resources, and common terminology.

## Scope And Era

The default setting is around `2016`, near the period of `False Value`. Use this as a gameable reference, not a complete account of real British policing.

Key assumptions:

- The UK does not have one national police force. Forces vary by region.
- London policing is mainly the Metropolitan Police Service, also called the Met, MPS, or the Old Bill.
- The City of London has its own police force.
- The British Transport Police covers the national rail network, including London Underground and overground rail services in London.
- The Met uses borough-based command structures during the default era. From `2017`, the `32` Borough Operational Command Units were reorganised into `12` Basic Command Units.
- The Folly/Special Assessment Unit is officially attached to Specialist Crime, but operates with unusual independence and behaves like its own Operational Command Unit.
- SAU officers can cooperate with other units across London; use that to keep routine paperwork and repetitive inquiries off screen.

Policing problems such as racism, profiling, discrimination, corruption, and misuse of powers exist in the setting and in real policing. Decide at Session Zero how directly the table wants those issues in play. If included, treat them as table-consent and tone issues, not default background colour.

## Met Structure

Specialised Met units include the Dog Support Unit, Mounted Branch, Specialist Crime Directorate, Economic Crime Unit, Specialist Firearms Command, and other teams. The Folly is officially hidden within this structure.

### Core Ranks

Most ranks up to Chief Superintendent can be prefixed with `Detective` when the officer is in an investigative role.

| Rank | Common abbreviations | Scenario use |
|---|---|---|
| Constable | `PC`, `DC` | Most numerous rank; first responders; can become specialists after probation. |
| Sergeant | `Sgt`, `DS` | First supervisory rank; briefs, assigns duties, and manages small teams. |
| Inspector | `Insp`, `DI` | Supervisory rank; DIs manage serious investigations and can be SIOs. |
| Chief Inspector | `Ch Insp`, `DCI` | Leads teams and major investigations; authorises queries and specialist resource requests. |
| Superintendent | `Supt`, `DSI`, `Det Supt` | Heads divisions within a borough command unit. |
| Chief Superintendent | `Ch Supt`, `DCS` | Overall boss of a borough command unit. |
| Commander | none listed | Senior management above borough level. |
| Commissioner ranks | `DAC`, `AC`, `Deputy Commissioner`, `Commissioner` | Senior public/accountability chain. The Commissioner is the Met's public head. |

Every attested police officer is legally a constable, regardless of rank. The attestation grants powers such as search and arrest under `PACE`.

### Other Police Roles

| Role | Scenario use |
|---|---|
| Special Constable (`SC`) | Part-time volunteer police officer with police powers and separate rank structure. |
| Police Community Support Officer (`PCSO`) | Community-facing liaison and visible deterrent; limited powers, no arrest power, no baton. Useful as local contact or translator. |
| Police staff | Civilian staff who handle administration, collation, analysis, and support work. Useful for moving paperwork off screen. |
| Forensic Medical Examiner (`FME`) | Certifies death at a scene and examines body/surroundings for evidence. |
| Scene of Crime Officer (`SOCO`) | Identifies, collects, and preserves physical evidence for later analysis. |
| Family Liaison Officer (`FLO`) | Supports and briefs families, helps investigators approach bereaved relatives and associates. |

### Training And Recruitment Notes

Circa `2016`, applicants need vetting and are generally at least `18`. The Initial Police Learning and Development Programme is a two-year route covering training and probation; later real-world routes include degree apprenticeships. Promotion requires assessment, exams/interviews, portfolios, and a further probation period.

Many Met officers commute into London rather than living where they work. This matters for local knowledge: PCSOs, community contacts, and local civilians may know a neighbourhood better than the assigned officers.

## Equipment

Uniformed patrol officers usually carry:

- Warrant card.
- Collar number on epaulettes for SCs, PCs, and Sergeants.
- Name badge.
- Custodian helmet, peaked cap, or bowler-style police hat as appropriate.
- Airwave radio.
- Mobile phone.
- Pocket notebook and pens.
- Body-worn video camera.
- Stab or ballistic vest.
- Torch.
- Extendable baton.
- Speedcuffs.
- CS or PAVA incapacitant spray.

Some officers may carry a stun gun. Most British police officers do not carry firearms; armed support normally comes from specialist units.

Plainclothes detectives usually keep a grab bag with:

- Warrant card.
- Reliable timepiece; practitioners benefit from a mechanical watch.
- Day book or pocket notebook and pens.
- Charged phone, charger, and spare battery if possible.
- Important contact numbers.
- Airwave radio and channel list.
- Torch and spare batteries.
- Witness statement forms and crime scene tags.
- Weatherproof clipboard and paper.
- Crime scene tape and evidence bags.
- Forensic gloves and protective clothing.
- Digital or physical maps.
- Practical weatherproof clothing.
- Food, drink, and physical money.
- Crime investigator reference material.

Uniformed officers seconded to the Folly normally switch to plainclothes for the assignment, though they may still need dress uniform for official occasions.

## Criminal Investigation Flow

A criminal investigation establishes what crime occurred, who did it, why, what evidence proves it, and what resources are needed.

Use this procedure as a scenario spine:

| Stage | Function | Game use |
|---|---|---|
| Crime instigation and call handling | A member of the public or officer reports the crime. | Delay matters: late reporting gives evidence time to degrade and suspects time to build alibis. |
| Response and management | First responders attend, preserve the scene, and begin control. | Establish the crime scene, who is present, and what can still be protected. |
| Investigation and suspect management | Detectives identify culprits, interview witnesses, interrogate suspects, and analyse evidence. | This is the main play stage for junior investigators. Keep dramatic work on screen; leave routine processing to NPCs. |
| Case management and court | CPS decides whether a case proceeds to trial; police support prosecution. | Usually background unless mundane court consequences matter. |
| Case disposal | The case is closed, left open, or recorded after court. | Folly cases often need non-court solutions because the culprit or cause may not be prosecutable. |

### Crime Categories

| Category | PIP level | Examples | Usual lead |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Volume crime | `1` | Theft, public order offences. | Usually a PC as Officer in Charge. |
| Priority crime | `1` | Force/community priority such as burglary or car crime. | Usually a PC as Officer in Charge. |
| Serious and complex crime | `2` | Violence, guns/knives, large financial crime, offences carrying `3+` years prison. | Usually DC, DS, or DI depending on severity. |
| Major crime | `3` | Murder, kidnap, or a serious crime escalated by public/media concern. | Requires a Senior Investigating Officer. |

CID or the Folly can become involved whenever circumstances warrant it.

## Crime Scenes

The Officer in Charge decides what counts as the crime scene. Scene boundaries depend on the crime, threat, evidence, and number of related locations.

The First Attending Officer is usually a PC. Their priorities:

- Identify themselves and establish authority.
- Preserve the scene by securing the perimeter.
- Create a single entry/exit point.
- Prevent evidence tampering or destruction.
- Identify victims and key witnesses.
- Enable specialists to collect evidence.
- Save life before preserving the scene if those duties conflict.
- Arrest a suspect if necessary and possible.

Additional attendees may include detectives, an SIO, FME, SOCOs, a photographer, briefing sergeant, and uniformed guards. An SIO rarely arrives first; they usually join once the seriousness is clear.

Investigators are more likely to be called in after first response because of specialist Folly expertise.

## Evidence

The `Golden Hour` begins when police are first contacted. Fresh physical evidence, accurate witness memory, and unbuilt alibis make this the best time to collect useful information.

After that, time becomes a resource. Requests, interviews, lab work, database searches, CCTV reviews, and stakeouts all consume time. Use time pressure as a case tool, but keep dull legwork off screen unless it creates atmosphere, tension, or a decision.

### Evidence Types

| Type | Examples | Common rolls |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Fingerprints, DNA, blood, fibres, wounds, objects, trace material. | `Observation` to find; suitable Expert skill such as `Science (Forensics)` or `Art/Craft (Forgery)` to analyse. |
| Intelligence | Witness statements, phone records, emails, police records, CCTV, contacts, victim history. | `Research` for records; `Social` to elicit information; `Read Person` to judge truthfulness. |
| Magical | Vestigia, supernatural pattern, demi-monde involvement, magically affected evidence. | `Sense Vestigia` and relevant Folly/magic procedures. |

Magical evidence may still be physical or intelligence evidence. It may also be useless in ordinary court, which is why Folly cases often need off-book resolution.

### Chain Of Evidence

Evidence is only court-safe if it was lawfully obtained, handled correctly, stored safely, and tracked.

Record:

- What the evidence is.
- When and where it was obtained.
- Who obtained it.
- Who accessed it and when.
- Who analysed it and how.
- Where it was stored.
- How it was preserved.

The Exhibits Officer usually maintains the chain. For non-court magical evidence, Folly investigators should still create an independent chain of custody, including storage and disposal decisions.

## Incident Room

Serious cases use an Incident Room or Major Incident Room as the investigation centre. It can be a physical room, a virtual workspace, or a HOLMES-backed case environment.

Use briefing scenes to:

- Recap player knowledge.
- Assign actions.
- Let the team discuss strategy.
- Have Nightingale, Peter, or senior police focus the investigation without solving it.
- Introduce new results from NPC legwork.

An evidence board is useful in play: victims, witnesses, suspects, places, objects, clues, and relationship lines. Use a physical or virtual board if it helps the table think.

## Investigation Techniques

### ABC

Use `ABC` to keep assumptions under control:

- Assume Nothing.
- Believe Nothing.
- Challenge/Check Everything.

This pushes investigators to test evidence instead of relying on first impressions, bias, or narrative convenience.

### Six Ws

Use the `6Ws` as a case checklist:

- Who was the victim?
- What happened?
- Where did it happen?
- When did it happen?
- Why did it happen?
- How was it done?

This expands the classic means, motive, and opportunity structure.

### TIE

`TIE` means Trace, Identify/Interview/Implicate, and Eliminate. It is useful when there are no obvious suspects or when an obvious suspect must be tested properly.

Build TIE categories from characteristics relevant to the crime:

- Appearance.
- Similar modus operandi or prior conviction.
- Relationship to victim.
- Access to scene.
- Presence near the scene at the right time.
- Residence.
- Vehicle access.

Populate categories from databases, records, statements, and interviews. Eliminate subjects through alibis, forensic comparison, timing, credible exclusions, or lack of opportunity. A non-eliminated subject is not automatically guilty; TIE narrows the field for deeper scrutiny.

## Magical Versus Mundane Investigations

The Met's arrangement with the magical world is vague outside senior officers and Folly-aware personnel. As magic rises, more junior officers know enough to recognise Falcon-related trouble.

Operational rules:

- Inform the SAU/Folly when a case appears odd enough to require Falcon assessment.
- Officers working with Folly investigators should seek Folly advice before acting on supernatural discoveries unless life is in immediate danger.
- Officers without Folly support should inform a senior officer and avoid further supernatural contact where possible, though some liaison officers may contact the Folly directly.
- Official reports should minimise explicit magical detail.
- Folly-only records should preserve the real supernatural account.
- Arrest and prosecution are not always possible. Folly investigators often need tact, compromise, secrecy, and practical containment.

## Police Powers

`PACE` regulates powers including stop and search, arrest/detention, evidence handling, and interviews.

### Warrant Card

A warrant card is police photo ID showing name, rank, and police service. Uniformed officers may not need to show it unless asked; plainclothes officers carry it separately. Suspension requires surrendering it. Losing it can become a disciplinary issue.

Civilian Folly consultant cards are weaker than warrant cards and do not grant police powers.

### Stop And Search

An officer may stop and question someone after identifying themselves and showing proof if not in uniform. The person does not have to answer.

An officer needs reasonable grounds to search a person, belongings, a home, or another private location. They must:

- Identify themselves.
- Say what they expect to find.
- Explain why the search is being carried out.
- Explain the legal power used.
- Offer a search record.

Search without reasonable grounds is only allowed in limited circumstances and requires senior approval. Searching private property usually needs a warrant unless life or property is in imminent danger. Evidence from an unlawful search may be inadmissible.

### Arrest And Detention

Any Met officer from constable to commissioner can arrest.

An officer needs reasonable grounds to believe the person is about to commit, is committing, or has committed a criminal offence, and that arrest is necessary. A warrant is not always needed, though some circumstances require one.

During arrest:

- The officer identifies themselves.
- The suspect is told the reason for arrest.
- The suspect is cautioned.
- Reasonable force may be used, including handcuffs if escape or force is a concern.

At the station:

- Name, birth date, and address are recorded.
- Possessions other than clothes and shoes are confiscated.
- Detention may last up to `24` hours before further authority is needed.
- The suspect may be questioned about the arrest offence.
- The suspect has legal-advice rights.
- They may notify others where they are.
- Food, drink, and medical treatment must be provided.

Arresting powerful demi-monde beings may be impossible, diplomatically dangerous, or pointless. Clear such action with Nightingale unless immediate life safety prevents it.

### Interviews And Interrogations

Informal interviews can occur outside police premises. Custody questioning after arrest is an interrogation.

Police-premises interviews use the `PEACE` model:

| Step | Function |
|---|---|
| Preparation and Planning | Plan topics, questions, closure, previous contact, and support needs such as interpreters or disability aids; get OIC approval. |
| Engage and Explain | Introduce officers, explain the interview, restate the caution, and explain next steps. |
| Account | Let the interviewee give their version without leading; then clarify and ask prepared questions. |
| Closure | Summarise the account, confirm understanding, answer questions, and explain what happens next. |
| Evaluate | Assess the information, plan follow-up, and review interview performance. |

Use the model to sell the setting; do not force the table through full procedure unless that is the fun part.

## Oversight

The Directorate of Professional Standards investigates Met officer complaints, fraud/corruption concerns, and professional conduct breaches.

Each police service has a Professional Standards Department. The Independent Police Complaints Commission handled serious external complaints during the default era; it was replaced in `2018` by the Independent Office for Police Conduct.
