# Case Design

<!-- Source: additional-rules, printed pages 336-342. Status: extracted-draft. -->

This file is GM-facing. It covers case construction, investigation flow, clue handling, location research, and campaign structure.

## Using Source Material

Short stories, novellas, crime plots, and folklore can be adapted into case files, but fiction plots need restructuring for play.

Do not run the novel sequence as a direct campaign unless the group specifically wants that. Prose fiction can rely on author-controlled timing, coincidences, intuition, off-page information, and rescue by powerful allies. A playable case needs an investigation the players can reason through from the table.

When adapting an existing story:

- Reduce the plot to essential locations, people, events, and supernatural causes.
- Convert the plot into a clue chain: what can be learned in each scene, and where each clue leads next.
- Identify the skill rolls that can reveal or interpret clues.
- Give important clues at least two or three discovery routes.
- Replace unexplained conveniences, sudden new powers, or metaphysical shortcuts with logic the investigators can test.
- Keep the investigators central. NPCs may provide expertise, but should not solve the case for them.

Stories noted as especially easy to convert into short, low-politics cases include:

- `A Rare Book of Cunning Device`
- `The Loneliness of the Long-Distance Granny`
- `The Furthest Station`
- `Body Work`
- `Black Mould`
- `Detective Stories`

`A Rare Book of Cunning Device` and `King of the Rats` are especially open-ended, so they work well as starting points for new answers, wider consequences, or campaign arcs. `The Cockpit` was developed into `The Bookshop`.

For a private home game, other crime or urban-fantasy plots can be used as inspiration. Change names, places, incidents, and clues enough that players who know the source are not simply replaying it, then add the magical element.

## Case Skeleton

Start with the antagonist or disruptive supernatural force.

Define:

- Who or what caused the problem.
- What they want.
- What they did.
- Why the Folly, police, or investigators become involved.
- What visible incident creates the hook.
- What would happen if nobody investigated.

An antagonist does not have to be morally evil. They may be hungry, frightened, defending themselves, protecting family or territory, serving a stronger power, protecting status or money, seeking revenge, trying to punish someone, or trying to force a change they believe is necessary.

Random criminality is usually weak material for investigation. A case becomes stronger when the investigators can infer motive, pressure, fear, appetite, obligation, or misunderstanding from the evidence.

## Hook Scene

The hook scene introduces the investigators to the case. Common hooks:

- An investigator encounters something supernatural directly.
- A stranger asks an investigator, Nightingale, or the Folly for help.
- A friend, relative, or contact asks an investigator for help with something strange.
- Another branch of the Met calls in the Folly because a crime appears Falcon-related.

Choose the start style deliberately:

| Start style | Use when | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Briefing start | The group benefits from context, time to gather equipment, and a clear chain of command. | Lower immediate impact. |
| In medias res | The scene itself should establish tone, danger, and urgency. | Players may need time during play to work out authority, jurisdiction, and context. |

A strong hook establishes:

- Atmosphere and location.
- Case boundaries.
- Key NPC relationships.
- Apparent method and threat level.
- Initial mundane and magical clues.
- Why this is a Folly/Falcon matter.
- Which specialists or police resources may become relevant.

Prepare a clue flowchart or relationship map before play. It should show where each clue can be found, what it points to, and how major NPCs, locations, and events connect.

## Investigative Scenes

Most of a case is a sequence of scenes: interviews, searches, research, surveillance, pursuit, confrontation, specialist consultation, and exploration of strange places.

Scene notes should include enough concrete detail to run the scene without locking the plot into one path. Some GMs need only keywords; others need fuller notes. Any improvised fact that could matter later should be written down immediately.

Investigators may visit scenes in an unexpected order, especially when following witnesses, suspects, and parallel leads. Keep the plot flexible:

- Move non-unique clues if the original scene is missed.
- Let more than one skill or approach uncover the same critical information.
- Avoid progress depending on a single failed roll.
- Let NPCs, photographs, records, or later analysis carry a clue across split-party scenes.
- If a clue requires a language or specialist skill, make the clue's existence obvious enough that the group can seek help, try Luck, or preserve it for later.
- Account for mobile communication, signal failure, and sanded technology when separated investigators need to share information.

## Antagonist Action

After the hook, decide what the antagonist does next.

Useful questions:

- Do they know the police or Folly are involved?
- Do they hide evidence, flee, escalate, attack, misdirect, or watch the investigators?
- Do they continue their plan on a timetable?
- What do they do if the investigators interfere successfully?
- What do they do if the investigators focus on the wrong lead?

Active antagonists make cases feel alive. Give them an agenda, then adjust their response when the investigators create new obstacles.

## Clues And Notes

Keep clear notes on everything: NPC names, places, improvised details, clues found, clues missed, handouts given, and facts the players have learned. Mark clues as discovered when they enter player knowledge.

Clue handling should be clearer than crime fiction:

- Flag clues more openly than a novel would.
- Essential clues should not be hidden behind rolls that can fail.
- A roll can decide who finds a clue, how quickly it is found, how much extra context is gained, or what complication follows.
- Nonessential clues, shortcuts, supporting evidence, and deeper interpretations can depend on successful rolls.
- Clues should be more obvious than real evidence because table cases resolve in hours, not months.

## Conclusion

The final sequence should follow from what the investigators have learned.

Decide whether the antagonist must be found, chased, lured out, arrested, negotiated with, exorcised, soothed, contained, or simply understood. Decide whether the antagonist resists, whether the investigators can walk away, and what emotional or physical pressure is present.

Crime fiction often ends with heightened danger even if real arrests are usually routine. A physical or magical threat can work well in the conclusion, but it should be scaled so the investigators have a fair chance to handle it.

## Folklore And Setting

The setting should shape the case, not just decorate it. The novels rely heavily on place, history, geography, local myth, institutions, class, transport, and neighbourhood texture.

Research the chosen location and use the results. Real places can provide crimes, rumours, architecture, access problems, social tensions, and supernatural hooks. Reality can also be changed when the plot needs a fictional estate, building, business, or local history.

Use folklore carefully. The game can include myth and supernatural traditions beyond the book's creature list, but avoid treating real cultures as exotic monster sources. Use respect, specificity, and local context; invented weirdness or local spirits may be safer than flattening a living tradition.

Useful research sources:

| Source | Case use |
|---|---|
| Local government sites | Services, contacts, planning issues, local institutions, civic problems. |
| Council and service reviews | Public frustrations, reputation, and local texture. |
| Local newspapers | Crime reports, civic disputes, events, recurring names, political context. |
| London Underground and transport maps | Travel logic, pursuit routes, timing, accessibility, and where civilians can plausibly go. |
| Street maps and street-view tools | Real layouts, access points, nearby rivers, alleys, parks, sight lines, and local atmosphere. |
| Postcode and property data | Demographics, house types, approximate wealth, floorplan clues, and raid/search aids. |
| Interior plans and virtual tours | Museums, galleries, public buildings, search logic, room relationships, and tactical constraints. |
| Online review sites | Visitor photos, unofficial details, complaints, and how a place looks when not professionally staged. |
| Travel guides | Area feel, food, transport, street rhythm, history, crime concerns, and practical maps. |
| Folklore and forteana sites | Ghost stories, odd events, urban legends, local monsters, and plot sparks. |

## Campaign Structure

A campaign is a linked series of cases with a theme or arc.

It can begin with one or two self-contained cases, then reveal connections:

- A recurring NPC.
- A repeated symbol, method, vestigium, or magical signature.
- Hints of a larger antagonist.
- Allies drawing the investigators into wider politics.
- Consequences from an earlier case returning in a new form.

Work backwards from the likely climax once it becomes clear. Identify:

- What the investigators must know.
- Which clues must be planted earlier.
- Which NPCs must be introduced, protected, replaced, or removed.
- Which objects, spells, resources, or relationships must be available.
- What earlier cases must establish for the final revelation to make sense.

Vary case types and tone across the campaign. Mix danger, research, emotional stakes, supernatural unease, humour, and memorable locations. Powerful beings such as genii locorum should feel unsettling even when they are not enemies.
